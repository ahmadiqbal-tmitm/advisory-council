# Tertiary Orality: Lessons from *Orality and Literacy* for AI Agent Product-Market Fit

*A context brief drawn from a chapter-by-chapter Socratic reading of Walter J. Ong's* Orality and Literacy *(1982), using a SuperSummary study guide. Written to help technologists test and sharpen new AI agent ideas.*

## 1. Why this brief exists

Every major communication technology has changed not just how people share information, but how they think, what they value and what they will pay for. Ong showed this for writing and print. AI agents are the next such shift. Builders who understand which human habits a new medium revives, suppresses or rewires will find product-market fit faster, because they will design with the grain of human behaviour instead of against it.

**How to use this brief:** read the framework (section 3), skim the insights (section 4) for the ones closest to your idea, then run your agent concept through the checklist and test questions (sections 5 and 6).

## 2. The book in brief

Ong's thesis: writing is a technology, the most transformative one humans have made, and it restructures consciousness. Oral cultures are not less intelligent; they think differently, because anything not memorable is lost.

- **Oral cultures** think in formulas, proverbs and stories; are conversational, communal, situational and combative; treat words as powerful actions; and quietly reshape the past to fit the present.
- **Writing** separates words from speaker and moment, bringing distance, precision, abstraction and analysis, and freeing the mind from memorising.
- **Print** fixed words in visual space: identical copies, indexes, silent private reading, individualism, ownership of words, and stories with tight endings.
- **Electronics** (radio, TV, telephone) created "secondary orality": a spoken, communal culture resting on literacy.

Ong wrote before the internet, smartphones, social media and AI. His history holds up; his picture of the electronic future is the part that has aged, and that gap is where this brief works.

## 3. The core framework: three oralities

| Mode | What it is | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary orality** | Cultures that have never known writing | Memory and conversation; formulas, repetition, live back-and-forth |
| **Secondary orality** | Ong's term for radio, TV and telephone culture | Spoken and communal, but built on literacy; mostly one-way |
| **Tertiary orality** (our working term) | Voice AI and agents | Two-way, responsive dialogue that adapts to the person, resting on a written core, delivered into a visually saturated culture |

**Working definition:** tertiary orality is the return of the oral tradition, supplemented by dynamic visuals. The spoken story is the anchor, carrying memory and meaning; the visuals move around it, adapting to the moment. You talk, and it shows.

**The visual-era caveat:** since 1982 a visual-digital layer (screens, images, memes, short video) has become a mode of its own. People now grow up fluent in speech, writing and visual shorthand at once. AI's return to orality lands on that blended culture, not the purely literate one Ong assumed.

## 4. Twelve insights and what they mean for builders

### Insight 1: We may be misjudging AI the way scholars misjudged Homer

For centuries, literate readers saw Homer's repeated phrases as clumsy padding, until Milman Parry showed they were the craft of oral composition. We risk judging AI by written-culture standards (original, fixed, sourced, linear) when many people use it the way oral cultures used conversation: quick, situational, reshaped to whoever asks.

**For builders:** before judging how people use your agent, notice which older medium's assumptions you are measuring it by. Usage that looks shallow or repetitive may be the product working as intended.

### Insight 2: Primary, secondary and tertiary orality

Radio and TV brought back only half of orality: listening without answering. Voice AI restores the dialogue, while its core stays literate (trained on text, speech converted to text and back).

**For builders:** the opportunity is not voice for its own sake but restored dialogue: products that adapt to the person in real time, as a good storyteller adapts to a room.

### Insight 3: AI as the village elder, and the risk of homeostatic memory

If people stop reading and remembering for themselves and ask an AI instead, the AI becomes the village elder. Ong calls oral memory "homeostatic": it quietly reshapes the past to fit the present. His example: the Gonja of Ghana told of a founder with seven sons for seven chiefdoms; after two chiefdoms disappeared, the story gave him five.

**For builders:** trust is the product. Users will rely on an agent that remembers, but only if it does not silently drift. Who decides what it remembers, and whose version it tells?

### Insight 4: Words as action, names as power

In oral cultures, words were deeds and knowing a name gave power over the thing: healers named plants, navigators named stars, reciters of genealogies held land. With agents, speaking becomes doing again: say a sentence and an email is sent.

**For builders:** this is oral-style speech with written-style consequences, since agent actions can be irreversible. Design confirmation and undo into the flow. Also expect today's "prompt specialists" to matter less as agents understand plain conversation; the lasting value moves from knowing the right words to knowing what to ask for.

### Insight 5: The Quran as a bridge between orality and literacy

The Quran's name means "recitation" and its first revealed word, "Iqra", means both read and recite. Millions memorise it verbatim and recite it aloud, with the written text as a fixed check: oral practice anchored by writing.

**For builders:** a strong model for trustworthy agents: conversational and spoken on the surface, anchored to a fixed, checkable record underneath.

### Insight 6: The agent and where effort moves

Plato warned writing would weaken memory. Writing didn't make people dumber; it moved their effort from remembering to analysing. The open question is where AI moves the effort. If it moves to judging, questioning and deciding, that's a gain. If it moves nowhere, that's Plato's worry come true. The hopeful version is that agents handle the logistics, and people spend the freed-up energy on the kinds of conversation that machines can't replace.

**For builders:** sustainable products move effort upward rather than removing it. A related risk: a fully anticipatory agent may remove the need to put wants into words at all, and putting thoughts into words is how people think. Keep the human articulating and deciding.

### Insight 7: Design is the visual layer of a tertiary orality

Print put words in visual space; desktop publishing (Adobe, then Canva) let everyone control it. AI makes design spoken: you describe it and it is laid out. Templates and brand kits work like Homeric formulas, recombined to fit the moment. Canva democratised making; AI democratises deciding, so taste becomes the scarce skill.

**For builders:** voice in, visuals out. Agents that pair conversation with generated visuals fit the emerging mode; the human role to support is judgement, not production.

### Insight 8: The written layer becomes plumbing beneath an oral interface

Indexes, page numbers and directories gave knowledge an address. With AI, retrieval returns to asking: "tell me" instead of "where is it?" The written layer remains, but out of sight.

**For builders:** most users never need to see the structure, but someone must always be able to open it. Make sources inspectable on demand without forcing them on everyone.

### Insight 9: Journey over destination, and the audience as producer

Print brought closure: tight plots planned on paper. Oral epics were open-ended journeys shaped by listeners. AI opens endless loops, and closure moves from the author to the person in the conversation. Consumer and producer become one (Alvin Toffler's "prosumer", 1980).

**For builders:** design for co-creation, not delivery. Let users steer, extend and end the experience themselves.

### Insight 10: Participation is a constant need; AI agents become a living manuscript

Hand-copied manuscripts absorbed readers' notes, so texts grew across generations. Print froze them. Social media revived participation at scale through reaction videos and memes. The need never changed; the form factor decides whether it shows.

**For builders:** AI extends participation from content to capabilities. People will add their own insight to an agent, publish it, and others will fork and tweak it, like open-source software. Agent marketplaces and remixable skills fit this need. The unsolved problem, as for the scribes: in a long chain of remixes, who gets credit?

### Insight 11: AI characters will grow round, but co-created

Oral characters were flat (Achilles always wrathful), which made them memorable; print produced round characters with inner lives. Today's agents are mostly flat: one clear role. Because context rules, they will have to grow round as they remember a person's history and preferences.

**For builders:** start flat for clarity and discoverability, then let personality deepen with context. Each user's agent will become a different character, shaped by that user.

### Insight 12: The story as anchor, visuals in motion around it

In oral cultures, stories were storage: navigation, law and lineage lived in narrative because people remember stories better than facts. The picture superiority effect shows images are remembered better than words, and Allan Paivio's dual coding theory (1971) holds that information stored both verbally and visually is remembered best.

**For builders:** an oral story plus visuals uses both memory channels at once. Teaching, onboarding and explainer agents should lead with narrative and support it with moving visuals.

## 5. Product principles checklist

1. **Conversation first.** Design for back-and-forth dialogue, not one-way output.
2. **Story as anchor.** Deliver knowledge as narrative and example, not bare lists.
3. **Visuals around the story.** Pair spoken or written answers with adaptive visuals.
4. **Anchored memory.** Ground recall in a fixed, checkable record so it cannot drift.
5. **Inspectable plumbing.** Hide structure and sources by default; reveal them on demand.
6. **Safe action.** Treat spoken commands as consequential: confirm, preview and allow undo.
7. **Move effort up.** Take the logistics; keep the human judging, deciding and articulating.
8. **Co-creation.** Let users steer, extend and close their own loops.
9. **Forkable by design.** Package agents and skills so others can remix them, and plan for credit.
10. **Flat to round.** Launch with one clear role; deepen character as context builds.
11. **Judge by the right medium.** Measure success by conversational standards, not document standards.

## 6. Questions to test an agent idea

- What human habit does this agent revive, suppress or rewire?
- Is it truly two-way, or secondary orality with a voice skin?
- What story does it tell, and what visuals move around that story?
- What does it remember, and how does a user check that memory has not drifted?
- Which actions are irreversible, and how are they confirmed?
- Where does the user's effort move: upward to judgement, or nowhere?
- Can a user steer, extend and end the experience themselves?
- Could someone fork it, adapt it and share it back, and who would get credit?
- Does it still need users to know "the right words", and how long will that last?
- Who is left out: by language, by access, or by skill?

## 7. Market note: the AI skills divide

Ong replaced "civilised versus primitive" with "oral versus literate" to describe cultures without ranking them. A new divide is forming around AI: not only which languages AI speaks well, but who knows how to use it at all, including how to talk to AI, prompt, set up connectors and plugins, create agents, and even which tools exist.

This gap matters most in countries newer to the internet, such as Pakistan, where many people are moving straight into an AI-mediated world. Two implications:

- **Voice can narrow the gap.** For the first time, people can reach a large store of knowledge without reading, provided their languages are well represented.
- **Upskilling is a market.** Teaching people and organisations how to work with agents (workshops, guided setup, implementation support) is itself an opportunity, alongside building the agents.

## 8. Risks to design against

- **Homeostatic drift:** an agent quietly reshaping facts to fit current training or user expectations.
- **Loss of articulation:** anticipatory agents removing the need to put wants into words.
- **Connected to minds, not people:** AI widens access to ideas while potentially reducing human contact.
- **Presence:** the voice returns, but there may be no one behind it. Many users will feel this as imitation, not communication.
- **Irreversible speech:** spoken commands with real-world consequences.
- **Credit and ownership:** remix chains without attribution.
- **Exclusion:** languages and skill levels left out of the AI shift.
